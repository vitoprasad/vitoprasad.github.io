The newest startups set up Claude before they buy marketing software

I read the public DNS records of 169 of the most watched startups, from the Forbes Cloud 100 and the LinkedIn and CNBC lists for 2026. Those records show which tools a company actually runs, which ones it is switching, and where its email is about to break. Sales teams usually pay a data vendor for those answers.

By Vito Prasad. Records read October 29, 2026.

Among the established companies on the Cloud 100, 86 run a marketing platform like HubSpot or Marketo and 80 have set up Claude. Among LinkedIn's top startups of 2026 it flips. 31 of the 50 have set up Claude, and only 23 run a marketing platform that shows up in their records.

My read is that the newest companies build their go-to-market around AI tools first and buy marketing software later, if at all. For a marketing platform, that makes more than half of the next generation a market still open, or one that may never come.

Spending data points the same way. On Ramp's AI Index, Anthropic took 60.5% of what businesses on Ramp spent on AI tokens in the week ending October 23, 2026. Across all 169 companies here, 128 have set up Claude, 111 ChatGPT and 94 Cursor. The typical company has three AI vendors, not one.

33 run two marketing platforms at once

That is usually a switch halfway done, or two teams that never agreed on one tool. OpenAI, Databricks and Notion are among them. Either way the decision is open now, and a sales team that knows can call this month instead of at renewal. See all 33.

19 are close to breaking their own email

Every tool that sends mail as a company takes at least one of 10 slots in the record that tells inboxes who may send it. Celonis, ClickUp, Armada and Metropolis are already past 10. Mail from any tool without its own signing key can now fail, and since 2024 Gmail, Yahoo and Outlook junk or reject that mail. Nobody gets a bounce. Replies just stop. 17 of the 19 run a marketing platform, so the sales and marketing tools are what filled the record.

The fix takes an afternoon. Drop the tools nobody uses. Have every sending tool sign mail with the company's own key, which has no limit. Send marketing mail from a subdomain like mail.company.com, which gets its own 10. Then check the count before connecting the next tool, which is a job for whoever runs GTM or marketing ops. Anyone can send email as 17 of them

Their DMARC setting watches spoofed mail instead of blocking it, so a fake email from Runway or Hugging Face lands like a real one. Fast-growing, famous companies are the ones scammers copy, with fake recruiter emails to candidates and fake invoices to customers. 15 of the 17 already get the reports and are watching. They usually stop there because blocking can break their own marketing email, and nobody has a full list of the tools that send as them.

That list is what this scan produces. Make every tool on it sign with the company's own key, then move the setting from watching to blocking over a few weeks. For a startup with a dozen senders it is part-time work, not a project. Here are the 17.

Why public records

A website crawl shows the tags on a company's pages. DNS shows the back office, who sends mail in the company's name and which AI tools it signed everyone into. It changes the day the work happens, before a press release or a job post, and every claim links to a record anyone can check. I built the first version at an AI email security startup, where it told sales which target accounts were changing their email setup, and sales worked those alerts.

How it reads a stack

Most go-to-market tools need a company to touch its DNS before they work. A landing page on Marketo needs a subdomain pointed at Marketo. A marketing platform that sends mail as the company needs a spot in SPF and a signing key. A workspace on ChatGPT, Claude or Cursor with single sign-on needs a verification record on the domain. Read those records together and the tools fall out.

For each company the scanner asks about 80 questions. It checks 45 common marketing and support subdomains, the mail servers, SPF and every service it includes, 27 common DKIM key names, DMARC, and the verification records on the main domain. It skips any subdomain that only answers because the company points every possible name at one server.

Two strengths are kept apart. In use means live mail or a live web page depends on the vendor today. Domain verified means the company connected its domain to the vendor at some point, which is strong for AI and work tools but can outlive a contract. Every finding links to the record behind it.

Some tools stay invisible. Anything that only runs inside the browser, like chat widgets and analytics tags, needs a page crawl. Tools that connect to a mailbox or a CRM through an API leave nothing in DNS. And a company can hide a tool behind its own servers. A quiet row means nothing was found, not that nothing is there.

This grew out of a monitor I built at an AI email security startup that read target accounts' email records every morning and told sales when one started changing fast. The email DNS check is the single-company version.
