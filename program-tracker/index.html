Engineering moves in weeks, regulators in quarters, and the launch date does not move

A first-of-a-kind hardware program slips when those three clocks drift apart and nobody sees it until a design review. This tracker puts all three on one row for every part of the system, flags drift weeks before it breaks something, and writes the review agenda and the Friday update on its own.

By Vito Prasad. Built from a product operations take-home I did in October 2026. The program and its data are made up.

The program is a small Earth-observation satellite with a fixed rideshare launch slot. It has eight parts, a 300 kg limit, two government licenses, and suppliers who need months of notice. Step back to week 6 and the tracker already flags propulsion, three weeks before it breaks its weight allowance. Then make something slip and watch the agenda change.

Four pieces, each replacing something

The readiness rows replace the three trackers each team already keeps. Engineering tracks weight in design reviews, regulatory tracks its filings in its own sheet, and supply chain tracks lead times in email. Put on one row per part, a license finding that adds weight or a supplier slip that eats the schedule shows up the week it happens. The numbers come from where the work already lives, so nobody types status.

The decision log stops decisions from being quietly argued again three weeks later. Each entry is one sentence that can be answered yes or no, a short reason, the alternative that was turned down, and the condition that would reopen it. When that condition comes true, the log says so.

The long-lead countdown measures each slow part against its need-by week. A four-week slip on a part with two weeks of slack is not a four-week problem. It is a missed launch slot.

The Friday update answers three questions in under a page. What moved, what is blocked and on whom, and what decision lands on leadership's desk in the next two weeks. It exists so leadership can skip the status meeting.

One meeting, and it only makes decisions

An item gets onto Tuesday's review only if it meets a trigger. A part is on course to break its weight allowance within six weeks. A license input is due within 30 days while an engineering question behind it is still open. A slow part has less than two weeks of slack. Or an agreed action is more than three days late. No general updates. Every item leaves as a decision with an owner and a date, or as an escalation with a written recommendation.

The cap is two hours of standing meetings a week for each team lead. More than that means the written pieces are failing and need fixing, not that the program needs another meeting.

The test at day 60 is simple. The first real weight breach or license slip should show up here and land on Tuesday's agenda before anyone finds it in a design review.
