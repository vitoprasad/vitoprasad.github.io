Hardware iterates in weeks, the NRC moves in quarters, and the landing slot does not move at all

A lunar reactor program slips when those three clocks drift apart and nobody sees it until a design review. I framed a hypothetical program around it for a product operations take-home in April 2026, integrating the heat rejection system for the LR-1 lunar reactor ahead of the SR-1 orbital test, then built the operating system I proposed so it runs.

By Vito Prasad. Built on public news about lunar surface power. The program status, its problems and its people are made up.

The system has to put out 100 kWe, reject heat in a vacuum, survive the 14-day lunar night, meet NRC Category II requirements, and fit a 15-tonne heavy-class lander. Every part trades against every other. A shielding change from regulatory eats kilograms that were set aside for radiator area, which was sized against a titanium tubing lead time. Step to week 7 and the tubing slip is already on the review. At week 8 the radiator is flagged six weeks before it would break its mass budget. Then make something happen and watch the review agenda and the Context Pack change.

Four artifacts, each replacing something

The Cross-Functional Readiness Matrix replaces the three trackers each team already keeps. Engineering tracks heat pipe and radiator maturity in design review notes, regulatory tracks its NRC dependencies in a separate tracker, and supply chain tracks fuel and titanium lead times in procurement email. On one row per subsystem, read the same week, a shielding change that adds mass shows up before engineering commits to a radiator geometry. Mass and maturity are pulled from the design and mass budget tools, not typed in.

The Critical Path Decision Log stops decisions from being quietly argued again three weeks later, after the hardware has moved on. Each entry is one sentence with a yes or no answer, a short reason, the dissenting view, and the data that would reopen it. When that condition comes true, the log says so.

The Long-Lead Procurement Burndown keys the ten slowest items to their need-by dates. A four-week tubing slip late in the year is not a four-week problem when it lands on the SR-1 test, because the 2030 landing slot does not wait.

The Weekly Context Pack replaces the status meeting. It answers three questions in under a page. What moved, with numbers from the matrix rather than narrative. What is blocked, and on whom. What decision reaches leadership in the next 14 days. Nobody writes status for it. It is built from the matrix changes, the decision log, and a short read of the week's design reviews and regulatory notes.

One meeting, and it only makes decisions

The Critical Path Review is the one meeting I would set up in week one. An item gets on the agenda only if a subsystem is on course to break its mass budget within six weeks, a regulatory design input is less than 30 days from its NRC gate with an engineering question still open, or a long-lead order has two weeks of slack or less. No general updates, no roadmap debates. Every item leaves as a decision with an owner and a date, or as an escalation with a written recommendation.

Standing meetings stay under two hours a week for each functional lead. More than that means the written artifacts are failing and need fixing, not that the program needs another meeting.

The leadership ask

The deck for leadership is four slides and asks for two binary decisions. Cut 8 kg from the radiator rather than release the reserve, because the reserve is the insurance against the shielding risk still open. Issue the firm-fixed-price titanium order now and qualify a second mill in parallel, rather than trade schedule slack for supplier options. Everything else waits for the monthly review. Within 24 hours a follow-up lists the decisions, the actions and their owners, and what evidence closes each one. When the matrix shows the change, the action closes.
