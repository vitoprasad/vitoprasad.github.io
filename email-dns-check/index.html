Email DNS check

What a company's email records give away

Every company publishes its email security setup in public DNS. Type a domain and this page reads those records live, names the vendors it can see, and tells you what my sales-signal monitor would have flagged.

Runs in your browser against Google's public DNS. Nothing is sent to me or kept on a server.

Watch list

The original monitor earned its keep by noticing change, not by reading a record once. Add a domain here and check back. The page compares each new read with the last one, using the rules my monitor sent to sales, and counts a change only when two reads in a row agree. The history stays in this browser.

Where this came from

At an AI email security startup, I built the first version as a Google Sheet and Apps Script. Every morning it read these records for 100+ target accounts, compared them with the last 30 days, and sent sales an alert when a company started changing fast. A company that moves from watching spoofed mail to blocking it inside two weeks is usually reacting to something. Sales worked some of those alerts, and our security engineers later built the idea into the audits they ran on prospect companies.

I also wrote the company's public guides on how these records work and on moving DMARC to reject without breaking mail. The writing told the market what to do, and the monitor told sales who was doing it.

This page is a fresh rebuild with the fixes I'd make. Only a record that starts with v=spf1 counts as SPF, because a parked record marked DO NOT USE once made my monitor log a month of changes that never happened. A change has to show up on two reads in a row. And the page names the vendors behind the records, which turns a target list into a map of who already sells to whom.

One blind spot stays. Tools that connect to a mailbox through an API, like Abnormal, leave no trace in DNS, so their customers look quiet here.

The rules

LevelChange between two reads
CriticalDMARC policy goes up (none to quarantine, or to reject) within 14 days. Or two or more changes inside 30 days, which looks like firefighting.
HighDMARC turned on for the first time. Mail routing or the DMARC report vendor switches, which usually means a new vendor. A policy rolled back, which usually means mail broke.
MediumSending services added or removed from SPF. SPF tightened or loosened. DKIM keys added or removed. Enforcement percentage changed.
