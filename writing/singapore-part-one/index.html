Part one of two

A Singapore address is the new Swiss account

Singapore will not enforce rules one foreign country writes alone, and for three years Washington and Beijing have been writing rules that look past a Singapore address to what they can reach. A Singapore address in 2026 is a Swiss account in 2008. Part one of two. Part two is about whose job it is to fix the leak.

By Vito Prasad. Draft, not published.

Working draft. The text and graphics are still changing.

On Monday the New York Times reported that a Commerce Department inquiry into Megaspeed, a Singapore company that bought billions of dollars of Nvidia hardware, stopped in 2025 after Nvidia's general counsel, Tim Teter, emailed the investigators working it. His email said Jeff Kessler, the Commerce official who oversees export controls, had cleared him to get in touch. The Justice Department asked Commerce to pause and now runs a criminal inquiry of its own. Megaspeed denies wrongdoing, and Nvidia says the diversion claims were investigated and debunked. Days earlier the Times and the FT had reported that Jensen Huang was in a relationship with Alice Huang, a former Megaspeed executive whom an American intelligence memo, as the Times described it, identified as recruited by China's Ministry of State Security. The two are not related. On Sunday Huang told Nvidia's staff in a memo that he had been unfaithful to his wife, without naming her.

The affair will get the headlines. Underneath it is a question about what a Singapore address is still worth.

On October 31 Singapore's Home Affairs ministry said the country "does not implement unilateral export controls imposed by foreign jurisdictions." The word to read is unilateral. Vivian Balakrishnan, the foreign minister, had explained it to Parliament in February 2025. "There are 200 countries in the world. It is possible for 200 countries to promulgate unilateral export measures. We have explained that we are not legally obliged to enforce that unilateral export measures of 200 countries." Singapore enforces its own law, to the letter. It sells the promise that what is lawful in Singapore settles the matter.

A Singapore address in 2026 is a Swiss account in 2008. Both are lawful to the letter, and both stopped settling the matter once a big power decided to look past them to something it could reach. Switzerland's turn came when Washington went past the account to the people. Singapore's has been coming one layer at a time for three years, and Megaspeed is the largest case yet. It is also the first in which Washington looked past the address and then stopped.

What Singapore sells

Singapore accepts every obligation it signs and none it is handed. Its new export-control order, in force December 1, copies the lists of the four multilateral regimes and adds none of the American-only chip thresholds. It has exchanged bank account data with other tax authorities under the shared international standard since September 2018, the same month Switzerland began. It declined a forced-labour import ban that would have cut its American tariff from 12.5% to 10%. What it signs, it enforces. What arrives from one capital, it does not.

Manus shows what that is worth to a client, and where it stops. The company was founded in Beijing in 2022 as Butterfly Effect. Around the middle of 2025, Nikkei Asia reported, it "quietly moved its headquarters to Singapore" and started recruiting locally, "while laying off most of its staff in mainland China." In December Meta agreed to buy it for about $2 billion and promised that "there will be no continuing Chinese ownership interests in Manus AI following the transaction." That was Washington's test, and the Singapore address passed it.

Beijing ran a different test. By March the co-founders were reported to be barred from leaving China. On April 27 the agency that screens foreign investment published one sentence banning the foreign acquisition of "the Manus project" and requiring the parties to withdraw. By June Meta had cut ties. In October Manus raised more than $500 million in a round led by Boyu Capital and IDG, with Tencent in it, CNBC reported.

Asked about it in July, Chee Hong Tat, a cabinet minister, said there was no need to comment "because no Singapore laws were violated." Whether a company leaves its home country "is determined by the government of the country concerned, whether China or the United States." Then he gave the pitch. "If you come to Singapore, you will find a stable and trusted environment. It is a base from which you can conduct business, raise capital and expand into the rest of the world." There is no official transcript, and the English reports translate his Mandarin differently. These lines are as reported by The Online Citizen. They describe the product exactly. Singapore offers a lawful base for paper and capital. It does not decide what happens to the people.

The layers fall one at a time

Washington's rules have moved one layer past the address every year since 2023.

The first layer was the registration. From November 2023 an American chip rule required a licence for any company whose parent is headquartered in China, wherever the company itself is registered. A Singapore subsidiary of a Chinese parent stopped counting as Singaporean for chips.

The second was the headquarters. The TikTok law of April 2024 ordered ByteDance to sell or be banned. It made no allowance for TikTok's Singapore headquarters or its Singaporean chief executive.

The third was the invoice. Nvidia billed $23.7 billion to Singapore in its 2025 fiscal year, 18% of its revenue, and shipped less than 2% of its revenue there. "Customers use Singapore to centralize invoicing while our products are almost always shipped elsewhere," its filing said. Tan See Leng, then a trade minister, told Parliament in February 2025 that Singapore's share "reflects the location where Nvidia's customers received the bill," and that in one quarter less than 1% of Nvidia's revenue was physically shipped into Singapore. In November 2025 Nvidia stopped reporting by billing address and started reporting by the customer's headquarters. In its restated figures, China's revenue for fiscal 2025 rose from $17.1 billion by billing address to $25.1 billion by headquarters. Nvidia does not say how much of the $8 billion difference had been billed to Singapore, and the word Singapore does not appear in that filing.

The fourth was the people. In March 2026 federal prosecutors in Manhattan charged a Super Micro co-founder and two others with selling at least $2.5 billion of servers to an unnamed buyer "elsewhere in Southeast Asia" and diverting more than $510 million of them to China. According to the indictment, auditors were shown "non-working, physical replicas" while the compliance auditor "was instead off-site enjoying entertainment paid for by" the buyer. The paperwork passed. What stopped the scheme was an indictment of people inside American reach.

Beijing skipped the first three layers. In 2023 it put a Singaporean executive of the due-diligence firm Mintz under an exit ban before raiding the firm's Beijing office, Reuters reported. With Manus it went straight to the founders' passports.

Megaspeed sits on every layer at once. It is registered in Singapore. Bloomberg, reading customs records, counted at least $4.6 billion of Nvidia hardware imported under its name into Malaysia and Indonesia, and found no evidence that any of it reached China. Bloomberg also reported that Megaspeed rents much of that capacity to Alibaba, which is legal, since Chinese companies may rent computing abroad but not own the chips. On Monday the FT reported that Alibaba, through a Singapore subsidiary, lent money to Megaspeed's Malaysian subsidiary in June 2024, and that Alibaba was the anchor customer of its regional cloud business. That is a Singapore company, its Malaysian subsidiary and a Chinese buyer's Singapore subsidiary, each lawful where it is registered. Washington began looking past all of it, and then the inquiry stopped.

Singapore designed the layer it reaches

Singapore's law reaches one layer, a lie told in Singapore. Re-exporting an American chip to China is not a Singapore offence, so Singapore has no reason to ask where a box goes after it leaves. The record that produces is clean by design. The minister's figure, less than 1% of Nvidia's revenue shipped into Singapore, is true, and it is also exactly what an invoicing hub would show.

When Singapore does act, it acts on its own law and, so far, after Washington has noticed. On January 31, 2025 Bloomberg reported that American officials were looking at whether DeepSeek had bought Nvidia chips through Singapore middlemen. On February 27 Singapore's police charged three men with lying to server makers about where their machines were going. K. Shanmugam, the home affairs minister, called it "an independent investigation conducted by Singapore." On August 13, 2026 the White House published a report naming Singapore among more than 40 economies used to get around American tariffs. The next day Singapore Customs charged a company and three people with falsely declaring China-made bedding and other goods as Singaporean. Each investigation may well have started first, as Singapore says. The charges came after the attention. I read that as the design working as intended. Singapore keeps the right to choose when its own law is used, and it uses it when not using it would cost more. That reading is mine, and no official has said it.

The scale is small next to what passes through. The port handled 44.66 million containers in 2025, most of them only passing through. The two publicised cases of faked origin on American-bound goods this year came to about S$25 million. Singapore's whole server-fraud case covers about $390 million of servers, against $2.5 billion through one buyer in the Super Micro case alone.

Washington's selectivity is worse, and it is in the same story. Commerce has not added a single company to its blacklist in more than a year, the longest gap in 18 years, according to the Times. The rule that would reach Singapore subsidiaries of blacklisted Chinese firms by looking through to whoever owns half of them was suspended in the truce with Beijing, and on Monday the suspension was extended to January 10. The Megaspeed inquiry stopped after Nvidia's lawyer intervened. Singapore chooses when to use its own laws. Washington is choosing not to use its own.

What the Swiss outcome predicts

Swiss banking secrecy was lawful from 1934. It ended when Washington stopped accepting the account and went after people it could reach. In 2009 UBS paid $780 million and handed over American clients' names. Congress passed FATCA in 2010, and the demand grew into the shared standard under which Switzerland, and Singapore, have exchanged account data since 2018.

The business survived. Swiss offshore assets grew 7.6% in 2025. But Switzerland's share of the world's offshore wealth fell from 28% in 2008 to about 18.7% in 2025, and Hong Kong caught up. The clients who did not need secrecy stayed. The premium for secrecy went.

If the parallel holds, Singapore keeps the port, the regional headquarters and the data centres, and loses the clients who paid extra for the address as a shield. The port grew 8.6% last year, so nothing is visible yet. The difference from 2009 is that nobody has finished the job. Washington wrote the look-through rules and suspended the one that matters most. Beijing looks through only when its own founders leave. Nvidia looked through in its own accounts.

For anyone buying, funding or clearing a Chinese-founded company that moved to Singapore, the working rule is to price it by the layer someone else can reach, not by the address. Ask where its paper, money, boxes and people sit, and which government can get to each. For Manus the answer was the founders, and it took Beijing one sentence. For Megaspeed it is still open, and the date to watch is January 10, when the rule that looks through ownership either returns or is suspended again.
