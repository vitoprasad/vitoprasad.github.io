The power problem is a casting problem

Gas turbine makers are sold out to 2031 and the price of a turbine has nearly tripled since 2019. Work backward from the chips and the constraint turns out to be a foundry, and the arithmetic says time is worth far more than efficiency. Part one of two. Part two is about who gets the blades.

By Vito Prasad. November 1, 2026.

If you are buying power for a gigawatt of AI chips, the price of a gas turbine has almost nothing to do with what it costs to make, and you should pay it anyway. A gigawatt of idle chips costs more than $400 million a month in depreciation alone. Against that, the whole premium for a fast turbine over a large one comes to about two and a half months of idle chips, and the fast turbine's extra fuel to about a week a year. Time is the thing being priced. The chips waiting on a turbine set what it is worth, and its price is rising toward that.

Work backward from the chips and the constraint is a foundry. Turbines need single crystal blades, a handful of plants can cast them, and a new one takes four to six years. That is the slowest step on the path a buyer controls.

This is the first of two pieces. This one works out the constraint and the arithmetic that prices it. The second follows the two companies building foundries to get around it, SpaceX and Boom, and asks who waits in the meantime, SpaceX included. The numbers are shown so you can check the reasoning rather than take my word for it.

What happened

The turbine makers are sold out into the next decade. GE Vernova ended the third quarter with 122 gigawatts of gas turbine backlog and slot reservations, which are deposits that hold a place in the production line, up from 83 at the start of the year, and said its full year order pricing per kilowatt is running about 50% above 2025. Siemens Energy's firm backlog is near 70 gigawatts. Mitsubishi Power told a Singapore audience on October 27 that a large utility-scale turbine ordered now arrives around 2031. Wood Mackenzie counts 110 gigawatts of orders against 60 to 70 gigawatts a year of manufacturing capacity, and expects the turbine itself to reach $600 per kilowatt by the end of 2027, up 195% from 2019.

The blade is the bottleneck inside the bottleneck. The hottest part of a gas turbine runs at 3,000 to 3,600 degrees Fahrenheit, above the melting point of the nickel alloy the blades are made of. They survive because each blade is grown as a single crystal around internal cooling channels, then coated. Howmet and Precision Castparts control roughly 80% of the world's capacity for those castings, and Howmet alone holds more than half of the industrial gas turbine blade market. On its October 29 call Howmet said the casting capacity it is installing now is already sold out, so new demand will be met only after 2028, and that its largest customers, the turbine makers, are sold out through 2031. That is how the two dates fit, on my reading. A large turbine ordered today waits in the turbine maker's queue until about 2031, and the queue cannot move faster than the castings already committed behind it. At an investor conference in September Howmet explained why new capacity is slow with one detail, that in a new casting furnace "what you see above the ground is only half of the machine."

The biggest buyer has started building the slow step itself. SpaceX confirmed in August that it will cast turbine blades and vanes at a plant in Bastrop, Texas. On its October 20 earnings call Elon Musk said turbine makers can deliver everything except the blades and vanes 12 to 18 months before they can deliver the blades and vanes. Boom Supersonic announced a single crystal foundry of its own on October 22, the day it opened a turbine factory and a month after losing the customer it built the factory for. Part two takes up both companies.

And the blade is now being sold separately from the turbine. Howmet's chief executive said on the same October 29 call that the plant SpaceX is installing in Southaven, Mississippi, just across the state line from Memphis, 33 Solar Titan turbines and 10 ProEnergy units, all runs on Howmet blades, and that "one Asian turbine manufacturing company has agreed to supply turbines without the blades" so SpaceX can fit its own, possibly derated until the castings match the original. I have not seen SpaceX confirm that. If it is right, a turbine has become a kit, and the kit is missing the one part nobody can make fast.

Work backward from the chips

Start with what physically limits each step, and how fast each step can grow.

A gigawatt of AI chips needs about 1.25 gigawatts of firm power once cooling and a thin reserve are counted, by my assumption. The grid cannot supply it on the buyer's schedule, because a new generator in the largest US power market now waits about eight years for its interconnection. So the buyer builds power on site. Cleanview counts about 82 gigawatts of such projects announced since the start of 2025, with roughly 2 gigawatts actually running by the middle of this year, most of it SpaceX's.

On-site firm power at that scale means mostly gas turbines. Gas engines and fuel cells need no blades, and they help. INNIO expects to deliver about 4.5 gigawatts of engines this year, Wärtsilä has sold more than 3 gigawatts to US data centers, and Bloom is building toward about 2 gigawatts a year of fuel cells. But they come in smaller units, Caterpillar is quoting its engines for late 2028 and 2029, and together they add a few gigawatts a year against the 82 gigawatts of on-site projects already announced.

A turbine's output is set by how hot you can run its first stage, and how hot you can run the first stage is set by the blade. Every other part of a turbine, the compressor, the combustor, the casing, the generator, is ordinary heavy industry with many suppliers. The single crystal blade is not. It is grown in a vacuum furnace from a ceramic mould that is destroyed after one use, at yields, the share of castings that come out usable, that fall as the blade gets bigger, in a handful of plants, to a specification the turbine maker has to qualify. Large land-based blades are the hardest case. Patent filings for the biggest single-piece castings report yields under 20%.

So the chain is chips, then firm megawatts, then turbines, then blades, and the blade is the slowest step on the path a buyer controls. The grid is slower still, at about eight years for an interconnection, which is why the buyer left it. On the path that remains, a turbine maker can add assembly capacity in two to three years. A blade line takes two years to install before a multi-year ramp, the vacuum furnaces alone take 18 months to two years to procure, and a foundry built from nothing takes four to six years and then has to be qualified by the turbine maker. The time to add blade capacity is about the same as the lead time the shortage creates. That is the signature of a constraint that cannot fix itself.

The arithmetic of waiting

Then ask who pays, and what they will pay. The arithmetic below is mine. The inputs are public and sit in the table at the end of this section, and on the site the table is a calculator, so move an input if you think it is wrong.

A dark gigawatt costs more than $400 million a month. Bernstein puts a one gigawatt AI data center at $35 to $40 billion, of which servers and networking are about $26 billion. Depreciate the $26 billion over five years and the chips alone cost about $430 million a month whether or not they have power, before a dollar of lost revenue.

Count lost revenue instead and the number is about ten times larger. SpaceX's finance chief said on October 20 that new compute is pricing slightly above $50 a watt, which I read as a year of revenue, so a gigawatt of chips left dark gives up about $4 billion of revenue a month. The two figures are two ways to price the same dark month, not costs to add together, because a chip that runs wears out too. The depreciation figure is the floor, and every buyer shares it. The revenue figure is what the highest bidder sees, and it is why that bidder can outbid the rest.

The whole premium for a fast turbine buys back about two and a half months. The turbines a buyer can get soonest are aeroderivatives, jet engines rebuilt as generators, at about $1,800 a kilowatt for the turbine alone by BNP Paribas's estimate in July. A large utility turbine of the kind built for combined cycle plants is about $950 a kilowatt by the same estimate, and one ordered today arrives around 2031. For the 1.25 gigawatts a gigawatt of chips needs, the fast option costs about $2.25 billion and its premium over the large one is about $1.1 billion, about two and a half months of idle chips. A buyer who can get power years sooner by paying that premium is right to pay it, and would be right to pay several times more.

Efficiency is worth almost nothing right now. The fast options are simple cycle machines, which burn the fuel once and throw the exhaust heat away. Aeroderivatives and used airliner engines bolted to trailers both run that way, at about 39% efficiency. A combined cycle plant captures the exhaust heat in a steam turbine and runs near 60%. At $3.50 gas the difference is about $11 per megawatt hour, which comes to about $105 million a year for 1.25 gigawatts running 90% of the time. That is about a week of idle chips. For as long as delivery time is the constraint, the inefficient turbine wins, and the market will look as if it has forgotten everything it knew about heat rate. It has not. It has done this sum.

That sets the ceiling price. If a turbine delivered a year earlier is worth a year of chip depreciation, it is worth about $5.2 billion for a gigawatt of chips, or about $4,100 a kilowatt of turbine. To a seller of compute earning $30 to $50 a watt, the same year is worth $19,000 to $32,000 a kilowatt of turbine at an 80% cash margin, and that arithmetic is mine too. A fast turbine sells for about $1,800, well under either figure. So the chips set what a turbine is worth, and the price is still climbing toward it. Buyers already pay for time where it can be seen. They pay about twice as much per kilowatt for a fast turbine as for a large one, and in the third quarter GE Vernova's cash flow got a $5.3 billion lift from down payments and slot reservation deposits, cash paid years ahead for a place in the line. The gap between what a turbine is worth to the buyer and what it costs to build is the margin available to whoever controls the slowest step. That is why turbine prices keep rising into a sold-out book, and why a buyer would build a foundry of its own.

Why the shortage persists

If a year of earlier delivery is worth two to eighteen times what a fast turbine sells for, why is nobody adding casting capacity fast?

Because the people who own the capacity are being prudent, and they have been right before. Howmet and Precision Castparts built ahead of demand in earlier cycles and wrote it down. Aerospace blades carry longer contracts and better margins than land turbine blades, so an aero order crowds out a turbine order in a fixed pool. And the orders they are being asked to build for can cancel. Crusoe, a data center developer, cancelled a $1.25 billion turbine order nine months after signing it, a story part two tells in full. A foundry is a two to six year bet placed against an order that can disappear in nine months. The rational supplier adds capacity slowly, and the shortage is the sum of rational decisions.

Memory chips are the same story with a different ending. High bandwidth memory, the stacked DRAM beside every AI accelerator, is made by three companies. SK Hynix has over half the market, Samsung and Micron about a quarter each. SK Hynix and Micron cut capital spending hard in the 2023 downturn, all three are now sold out, SK Hynix through 2029 by its own account, and new fabs do not reach volume until 2027 or 2028. The product has the same crowding-out effect too. A gigabyte of high bandwidth memory takes three to four times the wafer capacity of ordinary DRAM, so ordinary DRAM contract prices roughly doubled in the first quarter of 2026 and kept rising, the way aero blade orders crowd land turbine blades out of the same furnaces. What differs is the fix. Nobody building accelerators built a memory fab. The buyers signed multi-year supply agreements instead, Micron has more than 75% of next year's output locked across 26 of them, and the suppliers expanded against committed demand. That works when the supplier is large enough to fund the fab once the demand is firm. Howmet's entire gas turbine business is about a billion dollars of revenue, and the order it would expand against can be cancelled in nine months. When the owner of the slowest step cannot or will not fund it, the buyer has two choices, commit capital to the supplier or build the step itself. Memory makers got the commitments, and casting is getting the buyer's own foundry.

The way to make a foundry a safe bet is to own the demand on both sides of it. A captive customer for the turbines on one side, a captive use for the castings on the other. That is a narrow description, and a rocket company and a supersonic airplane company happen to fit it, which is the subject of part two.

The foundry is the asset. The turbine is the reason to build one. If you can cast the blade, you can sell it to turbine makers who have everything except blades, which is exactly the arrangement Howmet described. If you cannot, you are a turbine assembler standing in the same queue as everyone else. Howmet's chief executive made the point without meaning to when he noted that the Southaven bypass fleet runs on his blades.

The case against

GE Vernova says turbines are not the gate. Its chief executive argues that permitting is, and that 80% of its backlog is utilities replacing old plants, not data centers. If he is right, this is a utility replacement cycle that data centers happen to be bidding into. I think both are true. The utility backlog fills the factories, and the data center buyer is the marginal customer who sets the price, which is how prices rise 50% in a year while the customer mix barely moves. On permits, one tracker counts $68 billion of data center projects blocked by local opposition in the second quarter alone, and a gas plant on site needs an air permit. If the permit takes longer than the blade, the blade is not the constraint.

The slow step may not be the blade at all. When Musk posted in August that about 15 gigawatts of the compute built in 2027 would stay dark, a figure he called a consensus estimate, he blamed transformers, wiring, liquid cooling, chillers and networking as much as power. A transformer could turn out to be the step that expands slowest, in which case a foundry speeds up the wrong thing.

Engines and fuel cells may route around the blade. They need no single crystal casting, and a buyer who can use them skips the turbine queue. If their makers grow faster than the casters, the blade stops being the slowest step for those buyers, and what an early turbine is worth falls toward what an engine costs. So far the engine makers are booked into 2028 and 2029 as well, which says they have a slow step of their own.

Fifty dollars a watt may not last. Every number above the depreciation floor rests on SpaceX's own price for new compute. Most buyers earn far less from a watt, $9 to $16 by Bank of America's and Oracle's estimates, and SpaceX itself realized about $20 last quarter. At $9 to $20 a watt, a year of earlier delivery is worth about $5,800 to $12,800 a kilowatt of turbine at the same margin. The ceiling falls by about two thirds and stays above the depreciation floor.

The demand could stop. This is the strongest objection. Every number in the arithmetic rests on new chips arriving at $26 billion a gigawatt. If chip spending slows, fewer new gigawatts wait for power, what an early turbine is worth falls toward what it costs, new foundries are stranded, and the prudent incumbents will have been right again. Howmet's caution is the memory of exactly that cycle. Two parts of the argument survive a slowdown. The chips already bought still need power, and 82 gigawatts of on-site projects announced against about 2 running is a backlog that a pause in new chip orders would shrink but not clear. And Bernstein's own warning, that depreciation rather than construction is the risk, cuts the other way, because a chip that loses its value faster makes each dark month cost more. At a three-year life the calculator puts a dark gigawatt near $720 million a month. What does not survive is the ceiling on new turbines, which would fall with the orders behind it.

What would change my mind

  1. Howmet raises its gas turbine outlook in February and announces a greenfield single crystal line. That would mean the incumbents have stopped being cautious, and the opening for entrants closes.
  2. Turbine prices stop rising while lead times stay at five years. That would mean price is not clearing on the value of time, and the ceiling argument is wrong.
  3. A large buyer discloses a transformer or chiller lead time longer than its turbine lead time. That would move the slowest step, and the argument with it.
  4. A hyperscaler cuts its chip spending guidance. That removes the numerator from every sum above.

What a buyer does now

  1. Pay for the delivery date. A gigawatt of chips waiting on power loses about $430 million a month. A slot reservation, a used fleet or a rental that arrives a year sooner is worth up to about $4,100 a kilowatt on that alone, more than twice what a fast turbine sells for.
  2. Treat simple cycle as the bridge. The fast turbine's extra fuel costs about a week of idle chips a year. Run it now, and plan the steam turbine that turns it into combined cycle once power is flowing.
  3. Bid engines and fuel cells alongside turbines. They need no blades. Their makers are booked into 2028 and 2029 too, but a site that can take more than one kind of machine is less exposed to any one queue.
  4. Budget for the blades you will buy again. A fast turbine run at baseload needs a new hot section about every three years, and the replacement blades come out of the same sold-out furnaces. Book the overhaul when you book the turbine.
  5. Match the turbine commitment to the customer contract. If your compute customer can leave on 90 days' notice and your turbine agreement runs to 2029, you hold a firm order against someone else's option, which is how Boom lost Crusoe.

The slowest step sets the price

A fast turbine sells for about $1,800 a kilowatt. Delivered a year early, it is worth about $4,100 a kilowatt to a buyer who counts nothing but idle chips, and far more to one who counts lost revenue. That gap closes in one of two ways. Prices rise until buyers stop paying, or more blades get cast. The incumbents are adding casting capacity slowly, so for now prices rise, and the buyers who cannot wait start looking at a foundry of their own. Who builds one, and who waits, is part two.
