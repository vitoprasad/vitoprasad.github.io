Who waits for the blades

SpaceX expects about 13 gigawatts of compute by the end of 2027, and it is building a blade foundry. Boom built a turbine factory and lost the customer it built it for. Run the numbers and not even the highest bidder can buy enough fast turbines for 2027. The blade rations everyone, SpaceX included. Part two of two.

By Vito Prasad. November 1, 2026.

Part one worked out what the chips waiting on a fast gas turbine make it worth, and why the slowest step on the path a buyer controls is a cast nickel blade. This piece follows the two companies that decided to make the blade themselves, and the data center developer that walked away from one of them, because between them they show what the constraint does to the people inside it.

Two foundries

SpaceX is building its own. In late August Elon Musk confirmed that SpaceX will cast blades and vanes for natural gas turbines at a plant in Bastrop, Texas, next to its Starlink factory, on about 830 acres it bought quietly over the spring. His reason, in his words, was that "the limiting factor for nat gas turbine production is casting the blades & vanes." He claimed it could bring turbines online 18 months sooner. xAI, now inside SpaceX, runs about 1.7 gigawatts of gas turbines at Southaven, Mississippi, and has disclosed about $2.9 billion of turbine purchases, a $925 million agreement running through 2029 and a $2 billion purchase of mobile turbines in April. On SpaceX's October 20 earnings call Musk said turbine makers can deliver everything except the blades and vanes 12 to 18 months before they can deliver the blades and vanes, which is the gap he says the Bastrop foundry will close. The buyer of the turbines is building the part the turbines wait on.

Boom is building one too, and lost its customer. On September 25 Crusoe ended the launch order for 29 of Boom Supersonic's 42 megawatt Superpower turbines. Crusoe said its plans had not changed and it still intends to use turbines, "just not Boom's." On October 22 Boom opened a $150 million turbine factory in Colorado anyway, shipped its first complete turbine core to a test site, and announced an all-digital single crystal foundry of its own, with first castings expected in 2027. It has not named a replacement customer. Boom had already placed firm orders with Baker Hughes for 31 generators to pair with the turbines Crusoe no longer wants.

Both fit the one description that makes a foundry a safe bet. Part one said the way to build a foundry against orders that can cancel is to own the demand on both sides of it. SpaceX already casts superalloy parts for Raptor turbopumps, and Morgan Stanley's Adam Jonas noted in August that the rocket castings and the turbine airfoils use the same alloys and the same furnaces. SpaceX also owns xAI, the largest single buyer of on-site turbines in the country. The foundry has a captive customer on one side and a captive supplier on the other. Boom's version is the same shape. Symphony, its airliner engine, shares about 80% of its hardware with the Superpower turbine, so one blade line serves both.

Thirteen gigawatts against the fast-turbine market

Musk's own plan runs straight into the constraint. He said on the October 20 call that he would be surprised to end 2027 with less than 13 gigawatts of compute, about 10 more than SpaceX expects to have this December. SpaceX counts compute by the chips' own draw, which leaves out cooling, so a gigawatt of it needs about 1.25 gigawatts of firm supply by my assumption. Colossus 2 runs about 87% on its own gas today. At that share, 10 more gigawatts of compute is roughly 11 gigawatts of new turbines inside a single year. The arithmetic is mine, and the inputs are in the table. The table runs three gas shares. The lowest is the split Brian Wang, who writes the NextBigFuture blog, proposed on the InvestAnswers podcast, with the grid, solar and batteries supplying the rest.

The 2 to 4.5 gigawatts in the table is my count. About 1.9 of it is five 380 megawatt Doosan turbines that an industry estimate ties to SpaceX. The rest is whatever the $2 billion of mobile turbines and the $925 million agreement buy, up to about 2.6 gigawatts more if most of it is new and bought near $1,100 a kilowatt, and close to nothing new if the mobile turbines turn out to be APR Energy's fleet, which already powers today's compute.

The fast-turbine makers build about 5 to 8 gigawatts a year between them. GE Vernova's aeroderivative line shipped 13 units in the third quarter, about 2 gigawatts a year at that pace. Solar Turbines builds roughly 0.6 gigawatts and Caterpillar plans to multiply that by two and a half. ProEnergy's rebuilt jet engines run to about a gigawatt. FTAI's conversions of airliner engines reach about 2.5 gigawatts at full ramp, and more than a hundred of them are already under contract for 2027 and 2028. Added up, that is about 6 gigawatts a year with FTAI at full ramp, and 7 once Caterpillar's expansion lands, before Siemens Energy's and Mitsubishi's smaller machines, which I could not size, so call it 5 to 8. Caterpillar is quoting late 2028 for new orders. Large utility turbines are sold out to 2031. So the plan needs between 1.4 and 2.2 times everything the fast makers build in a year, and SpaceX's disclosed turbine spending, $2 billion plus $925 million, buys about 1.5 to 3 gigawatts at $1,000 to $2,000 a kilowatt. Musk called the rest of the plan "highly proprietary."

There are four ways it could still happen. SpaceX may already hold machines for most of the gap that it has not disclosed. The grid may supply about 40% of the new load, as Wang's split assumes, which is 4 to 5 gigawatts of new utility service in 14 months against a record of about half a gigawatt in two years from TVA and the Memphis utility. Engines and fuel cells, which need no blades, may fill the gap, though they are booked into 2028 and 2029 too. Or SpaceX may buy most of the world's 2027 output of fast turbines plus whatever used fleets are for sale, which is what the April purchase and the acquisition of APR Energy's fleet look like the start of.

The fourth way cannot close the gap on its own. At the 87% gas share the gap is 6.4 to 8.9 gigawatts, as much as or more than all the fast-turbine makers build in a year, and much of 2027 is already spoken for, with FTAI's conversions under contract into 2028 and Caterpillar quoting late 2028 for new orders. Even buying every uncommitted fast turbine leaves SpaceX short unless the grid, engines or machines it has not disclosed cover the rest. So the blade shortage rations the highest bidder too. SpaceX can pay the most, at $30 to $50 a watt of revenue, and will likely wait the least, but on the public numbers its 2027 target slips, and every fast turbine it does take is one another buyer waits for. The connection is mine.

Musk's own count of dark chips is not a blade number. He posted in August that about 15 gigawatts of the compute built in 2027 cannot be switched on in 2027, a figure he called a consensus estimate, and linked a Grok conversation as its source. He put the delay on transformers, wiring, cooling and networking as much as on power. The figure shows how much compute may wait. It does not show how much of the waiting is the blade's.

Consensus estimate is that ~15GW of AI compute produced in 2027 cannot be turned on in 2027.

Baseload eats blades. Aeroderivatives were built for peaking duty. Run flat out, a hot section lasts about 25,000 hours, a little over three years at 90% of the time, so a fleet of 200 to 300 of them needs roughly 5,000 to 8,000 new first-stage blades a year just to keep running. That is my inference from the service intervals, and it gives the Bastrop foundry a captive replacement market even if new turbine orders stop. It is a floor under the foundry beyond rocket parts.

What turned out differently

A contracted backlog is still a milestone. Hard tech investors have spent three years learning to pay for artifacts rather than milestones, a part with a number that a customer with a budget pays for this year, and in September Boom's $1.25 billion of contracted turbine orders from Crusoe looked like the cleanest artifact in the sector. On September 25 Crusoe cancelled the order. It had been an option Crusoe held on a future where turbines were its primary power, and Crusoe's sites already had GE aeroderivatives and Caterpillar turbines for backup. When that future did not arrive, Crusoe let the option lapse. Boom, meanwhile, had placed firm orders on generators against it. As one write-up of the deal put it, the buyer kept the option and the supplier had already placed the order.

The tighter definition is that an artifact is something you have delivered to a customer who has paid. Everything before that is a milestone with better paperwork, and the better the paperwork, the easier it is to mistake for revenue.

SpaceX now sits on the supplier's side of the Crusoe trade. Anthropic, about a third of SpaceX's revenue last quarter, can cancel its SpaceX compute on 90 days' notice once the first terms end in early 2027, while its $110 billion commitment to Amazon cannot be cancelled. SpaceX's own turbine agreements run to 2029.

Buyers pay for certainty when time is the constraint. Boom's turbine was priced around $1,030 a kilowatt including the generator and maintenance, against roughly $1,800 for a GE aeroderivative package by one industry estimate. Crusoe stayed with the incumbent. It is not alone. NextEra has booked four gigawatts of GE Vernova turbine slots for its data center customers, and Crusoe itself is in a venture with Engine No. 1 to procure 4.5 gigawatts from GE Vernova and Chevron. When a month of delay costs $430 million, a turbine that has never run costs more than its discount once the delay is priced in. The core Boom shipped on October 22 has not fired yet. It is due to fire before Thanksgiving. Until it does, Boom is selling a delivery date, and Crusoe has just put a price on a startup's delivery date.

The case against

SpaceX's 18 months may be a decade. Howmet's scale, yields and technology took decades. The furnaces take two years to get. A greenfield foundry is four to six years plus qualification, and large land-based blades are the hardest castings there are. Casting a turbopump part is not casting a single crystal airfoil the length of a forearm. If the bladeless machines Howmet described are the 380 megawatt Doosan units linked to SpaceX, their first-stage blades are the largest single crystal castings made, which is the hardest case for Bastrop, not the easiest. A SpaceX blade spinning in a data center turbine is 2028 at the earliest, and the early 2030s if the yields behave the way yields usually behave.

Musk may simply get the turbines. This is the strongest objection. The four ways above are not exclusive, and the man has a record of buying his way out of queues. If he does, the 13 gigawatts arrive, the claim that the blade rations even the highest bidder is wrong for SpaceX, and the shortage shows up only on other buyers' books, in the fast turbines they could not buy. A reader who only watches SpaceX will conclude the blade was never the problem.

The permit may be the slowest step after all. Southaven shows what happens when a buyer treats permitting as optional. SpaceX is in federal court over turbines it ran there without permits and has agreed to remove them by July 2027. On October 30 the judge refused to dismiss the NAACP's case, ruling that calling the turbines temporary does not exempt them from clean air permits. A request to shut them down until they are permitted is still pending. The hyperscalers have signed a White House pledge not to push power costs onto ratepayers, and local opposition is now blocking projects by the tens of billions a quarter. If the permit takes longer than the blade, SpaceX will have built a foundry to speed up a step that was never the slow one.

What would change my mind

  1. A SpaceX-cast blade runs in a qualified turbine before 2029. That would mean greenfield casting is faster than the industry believes.
  2. SpaceX ends 2027 at 13 gigawatts, mostly on its own gas. That would mean the fast-turbine market, or SpaceX's undisclosed supply, was deeper than the public numbers show, and the claim that the blade rations even the highest bidder is wrong.
  3. Boom names a paying customer and delivers 250 megawatts in 2027. That would mean the market does pay a startup for a date after all.
  4. SpaceX reports new compute pricing below $20 a watt. That would cut the revenue ceiling in part one by more than half, though it would stay above the depreciation floor.

Who waits

The question for a hard tech company used to be what it could sell before the hard part works. The question now is what it can make that nobody else can make fast, because that is where the price goes when time is the constraint. In data center power that is a nickel blade grown as a single crystal, and the two companies building furnaces for it are the two with engines of their own to feed. A SpaceX blade is two years away at the earliest. Until then SpaceX waits on the same few furnaces as everyone else.
