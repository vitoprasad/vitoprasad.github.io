Part two of two

Whose job it is

Washington blames Singapore for leaks it has stopped trying to close at home. The last two times it stopped a leak like this one, it fixed its own side first and asked its partners to sign second, and Congress did it over a president's objection. Part two of two.

By Vito Prasad. Draft, not published.

Working draft. The text and graphics are still changing.

Part one ended on a question. If a Singapore address in 2026 is a Swiss account in 2008, someone has to finish the job, and this year Washington started and stopped. The easy answer is that the job is Singapore's. Megaspeed is registered there, the invoices said Singapore, and Singapore has said in Parliament and in writing that it will not enforce American rules.

The easy answer skips two things. Singapore is already paying for its position, in a currency Washington chose. And Washington's own side of the leak is open, by its own choices, in ways no partner can fix.

What Singapore pays

The United States runs a trade surplus with Singapore and a deficit of nearly $191 billion with Vietnam, and charges both 12.5%.

The rate does not follow the trade. It follows a law. The tariff comes from a US action against goods made with forced labour. Economies that ban imports made with forced labour, or have promised to in a trade deal, pay 10%. Malaysia and Indonesia made that promise in their agreements, and Canada, Mexico, the UK and India get 10% too. Singapore has no such ban, and the notice lists it among the economies that "have failed to impose and effectively enforce" one.

The price is small and Singapore pays it in public. The rate leaves out pharmaceuticals, chips, computers and most of what Singapore actually sends, so it lands on about a third of its exports to the US, according to reporting on Singapore's trade ministry. Gan Kim Yong, the trade minister, said in September that a ban "would have significant implications for Singapore's trade." Singapore would rather pay 2.5 points on a third of its exports than pass one law that someone else asked for.

The next step is my inference, and no Singapore official has made it. The two refusals have the same shape. For a port where most containers are only passing through, a forced-labour import ban means checking where goods came from, much of it China. Enforcing America's chip rules means checking where chips go next. Either way Singapore would be policing origin and destination on Washington's behalf, and the product it sells is the promise that it only polices its own. Part one called that accepting every obligation it signs and none it is handed. The tariff is what the second half costs.

What Washington chose not to do

Washington has the tools to look past a Singapore address, and in the past year it has put most of them down.

The blacklist is the strongest. A company on the Commerce Department's entity list is cut off from American technology wherever it is registered. Commerce has not added a single company in more than a year, the longest gap in 18 years, according to the Times, and other agencies proposed adding Megaspeed. The rule that would extend the blacklist to any firm half-owned by a listed one, wherever it is registered, was suspended in the truce with Beijing last November and extended on Monday to January 10.

The trade-off shows in the money. The White House announced it would take 15% of Nvidia's licensed China sales of one chip. Nvidia's filing says the government "did not publish a regulation codifying such requirement," and the sales it covered came to about $60 million. I read these facts as one policy. Export rules have become a bargaining chip in trade talks with Beijing, and a blacklisting would cost the chip. That connection is mine, and no official has said it.

Then there is the gap the rules were not built for. They mostly govern where a chip is shipped, not who rents its time. On October 1 the FT reported that Tencent had signed a five-year lease, worth about $7 billion, for access to around 100,000 advanced chips in Oracle data centres in Southeast Asia. No rule stood in the way. On November 4 the FT reported that Oracle had cancelled it after US officials raised concerns, and that officials in Guangdong had separately asked Tencent to support Chinese chips. Neither company has confirmed either report. If the FT is right, Washington stopped the deal the way it stopped Super Micro, by leaning on an American company within reach. It worked once, for one deal, and it is not a rule. The House voted 369 to 22 in January to make remote access an export in law. The bill has sat in a Senate committee since.

Even the rules Washington did write have not held still. The caps on chip shipments to Malaysia, Singapore and most of the world, written in January 2025, were dropped by the next administration in May before they took effect. It dropped them by press release, which the Government Accountability Office ruled this May "must be submitted to Congress and the Comptroller General before it can take effect." Singapore watched American chip policy reverse within four months. A careful government obeys a rule like that to the letter and waits it out.

It has been fixed twice before

Both times, Washington fixed its own side first and asked its partners to sign second.

The first time was the Cold War. American rules on selling technology to the Soviet bloc were never only American. From 1949 the United States and its allies kept one list through a committee in Paris called CoCom. In 1987 it came out that Toshiba Machine and Norway's Kongsberg had sold the Soviets milling machines able to cut quieter submarine propellers. On June 30 the Senate voted 92 to 5 for mandatory sanctions on both companies. The next day members of Congress smashed a Toshiba radio with sledgehammers on the Capitol lawn. Two weeks later Representative Helen Bentley, one of them, told the House, "Treachery by any other name is still treachery. But, if it had another name, it would be Toshiba." The fix came from Tokyo and Oslo. Toshiba's chairman and president resigned, and Japan and Norway punished the companies involved. They did it because the list they had broken was a list they had signed.

The second time was the Swiss account in part one. Washington did not wait for Bern to change its law. It enforced its own on a bank with a large American business, and UBS paid $780 million in 2009. Only then did the demand become FATCA, and then a reporting standard other countries signed, Singapore among them. Both countries have exchanged account data under it since 2018.

Singapore and Malaysia are not members of the Wassenaar Arrangement, CoCom's looser successor. Singapore's own control list does copy the four multilateral regimes, and its new order, in force December 1, adds none of the American-only chip thresholds. It enforces what it has signed. That is the opening, and Singapore has said so in its own words.

What would fix it

The fix runs in the same order as 1987 and 2009.

  1. Washington uses the tools at home. It decides blacklist cases on the evidence, not the trade calendar, closes the rental gap in law rather than by pressing one company at a time, and keeps charging the people it can arrest.
  2. It turns a unilateral rule into a shared one. Southeast Asia's data centre boom runs on American chips, which gives Washington something to offer as well as something to withhold. Guarantee the region's supply in exchange for joint checks on who the end customers are and shared customs records, written at a table where Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia and Thailand have seats. Singapore takes the ASEAN chair on January 1, 2027.
  3. Only then, it names the partners that sign and cheat.

For Singapore the same deal is a new product. It has spent three years selling distance, and part one showed the big powers pricing distance down. It could sell checking instead, a place where an invoice is lawful because someone verified where the chip went. Balakrishnan has already written the sales line. Singapore wants "to be useful to big and middle powers, but we will not be made use of." A rule Singapore signed would let it be useful without being used.

The White House may not do any of this. In 1987 it did not want to either. Reagan threatened to veto the whole trade bill if the Toshiba sanctions stayed in, UPI reported in April 1988. Congress kept them. When he did veto the bill in May, his message objected to a plant-closing rule and five other provisions, and did not mention Toshiba. Congress passed it again with a softened version of the sanctions, and he signed it in August 1988.

On November 3 Democrats won both chambers, and the House has already passed the rental bill 369 to 22. A rule passed by both parties would be the first American chip rule likely to outlast an election, and the first a careful government would find worth signing.

None of this requires Megaspeed to be guilty. It may well be innocent, as it says. The problem is that under the current arrangement nobody can tell, and the one agency that was trying stopped.

In 1987 the sledgehammers made the news. What fixed the leak was a list Tokyo had signed and a law Congress passed over a president's objection. Singapore has said in one word what it would take to sign this one.
